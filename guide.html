What is an Online Notability Score?

An Online Notability Score is a numeric measure, from 0 to 100, of how strong a person's, brand's, or company's public web and media presence really is, based on real, observable signals rather than a simple mention count.

It's designed to answer a simpler question than most PR software asks: not "how much media monitoring data exists," but "how notable is this entity, actually?" A higher score reflects broader, more independent, higher-quality coverage — not just a higher volume of links.

How is a Notability Score calculated?

It's calculated from six weighted signals: media presence, source quality, coverage diversity, recency, originality, and entity consistency, not just how many times a name appears online.

Media presence measures how much legitimate third-party coverage exists at all. Source quality weighs how authoritative and independent those sources are. Coverage diversity checks whether mentions span multiple outlets and topics, rather than one echo. Recency looks at how current the strongest coverage is. Originality separates independent articles from duplicated or syndicated copies. Entity consistency checks whether different sources agree on who or what is being described.

Does having more articles about me make me more notable?

No, quantity alone doesn't determine notability. A handful of independent, high-authority articles typically outweighs dozens of duplicated or syndicated copies of the same story.

This is one of the most common misconceptions in online reputation. A press release picked up by 40 low-authority aggregator sites is one story, not 40 stories. Genuine notability comes from independent editorial judgment: multiple, unrelated publications deciding on their own that something is worth covering.

What's the difference between independent coverage and syndicated coverage?

Independent coverage is an original article written by a specific publication. Syndicated coverage is the same underlying story republished across multiple outlets, so it shouldn't be counted as separate proof of notability.

Detecting this distinction is one of the harder technical problems in measuring notability, since surface-level mention counts treat every copy as a new, separate signal, inflating scores for entities that simply pushed one press release widely rather than earning varied coverage.

How can I increase my online notability?

The most reliable way is to earn independent editorial coverage across multiple credible, unrelated publications, rather than relying on one channel, paid placements, or repeated self-published content.

In practice, that usually means: pitching a genuinely newsworthy angle to more than one type of publication (not just one industry blog), building a consistent public identity so sources describe you the same way, and being patient. A track record built over time across varied sources reads as far more credible than a short burst of activity from one source.

What is source quality or source authority in PR and SEO?

Source quality refers to how credible, established, and editorially independent a publication is. A mention in a well-known, independent outlet generally carries more weight than a mention on a low-authority or promotional site.

This is why two entities can have the same raw mention count and end up with very different notability scores: one might be covered by respected, independent publications, while the other's mentions come mostly from low-authority directories or paid placements.

How does AI like ChatGPT or Gemini decide what it knows about me?

AI systems largely learn from what's published and indexed across the web, so consistent, accurate, and independently verified information across multiple sources makes it more likely an AI will describe you accurately when asked.

This is the emerging link between traditional press authority and "AI visibility": entities with a stronger, more consistent public web presence tend to be represented more accurately and more often in AI-generated answers, since those answers are shaped by what's been written about you, by whom, and how consistently.

What does it mean if my coverage isn't diverse?

It means most of your mentions are concentrated in one publication, topic, or category, which can make an online presence look narrower or less established than one built across multiple credible sources.

For example, a founder covered only in startup-funding roundups, with no coverage of their product, expertise, or industry perspective, has a narrower public identity than one covered across multiple angles by multiple outlet types.
